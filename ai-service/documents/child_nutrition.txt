CHILD NUTRITION IN THE FIRST 1000 DAYS
UNICEF & WHO Guidelines for Sub-Saharan Africa

THE FIRST 1000 DAYS
The first 1000 days of a child's life — from conception to the second birthday — represent the most critical window for nutrition. Adequate nutrition during this period determines a child's physical growth, cognitive development, immune function, and lifelong health. Malnutrition during this period causes irreversible damage.

EXCLUSIVE BREASTFEEDING (0-6 MONTHS)
Every child should receive ONLY breast milk for the first 6 months of life. No water, formula, or other foods should be given. Breast milk contains all the nutrients, antibodies, and growth factors needed. It protects against diarrhea, pneumonia, and other infections that kill children. In Mauritania and the Sahel, exclusive breastfeeding rates remain below WHO targets.

Benefits of exclusive breastfeeding:
- Provides complete nutrition for 6 months
- Contains maternal antibodies that protect against infection
- Reduces risk of diarrhea by 50% and pneumonia by 72%
- Lowers risk of overweight and diabetes later in life
- Promotes mother-child bonding

COMPLEMENTARY FEEDING (6-24 MONTHS)
At 6 months, breast milk alone is no longer sufficient. Introduce complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding until 24 months or beyond.

Key principles of complementary feeding:
- TIMELY: Start at exactly 6 months, not before, not much after
- ADEQUATE: Sufficient energy, protein, and micronutrients
- SAFE: Clean preparation, no contamination
- RESPONSIVE: Feed in response to hunger cues

What to feed children 6-24 months:
- Cereals: millet, sorghum, rice, maize porridge (fortified when possible)
- Legumes: lentils, beans, cowpeas (excellent protein source)
- Animal products: eggs, meat, fish, milk (high-quality protein and zinc)
- Orange/yellow vegetables: sweet potato, carrot, pumpkin (vitamin A)
- Dark leafy vegetables: moringa leaves (iron, calcium, vitamins)
- Fruits: banana, mango, papaya (vitamins, energy)

Meal frequency by age:
- 6-8 months: 2-3 meals/day + breastfeeding
- 9-11 months: 3-4 meals/day + breastfeeding + 1-2 snacks
- 12-24 months: 3-4 meals/day + breastfeeding + 1-2 snacks

MORINGA — MAURITANIA'S NUTRITIONAL TREASURE
Moringa oleifera leaves are exceptionally nutritious and widely available in Mauritania. Fresh moringa leaves contain:
- 7x more vitamin C than oranges
- 4x more calcium than milk
- 4x more vitamin A than carrots
- 2x more protein than yogurt
- High iron content for anemia prevention

Moringa can be added to porridges, soups, and sauces. Drying moringa preserves most nutrients and makes it available year-round.

VITAMIN A DEFICIENCY
Vitamin A deficiency affects millions of children in West Africa and increases mortality risk. Signs include night blindness and increased susceptibility to infections. Sources: liver, eggs, orange/yellow produce, moringa. UNICEF provides vitamin A supplementation twice yearly during Child Health Days.

IRON DEFICIENCY ANEMIA
Anemia is prevalent in Mauritanian children. Iron-rich foods: red meat, liver, legumes, dark green leaves, millet. Vitamin C enhances iron absorption — serve vitamin C foods alongside iron-rich foods.

ZINC AND DIARRHEA
Zinc is critical for immune function and growth. Zinc supplementation during diarrhea episodes reduces duration and severity. Food sources: meat, legumes, seeds, whole grains.

SIGNS OF ADEQUATE NUTRITION
- Child is active and alert
- Regular weight gain (approximately 500g/month in first 6 months)
- Normal MUAC for age
- No swelling of hands, feet, or face
- Bright eyes, shiny hair
- Good appetite
